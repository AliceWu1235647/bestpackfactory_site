Custom Tissue Paper Artwork Guide: Repeat Pattern, Ink Coverage, Colours and Show-Through

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Design custom tissue paper as a spaced, seamless repeat pattern rather than a single centred logo, because the sheet is folded and crumpled and only parts of it are seen. Keep ink coverage light, use one or two flat spot colours, avoid gradients, photos, hairlines and tiny text, and expect dark inks to show through to the other side of thin tissue. Ask the printer for the repeat tile size and coverage limit before you start, and approve a printed sample.

1. Start from the repeat, not the sheet

Custom tissue is commonly printed on a reel and then cut into sheets, so the design is built as a tile that repeats in both directions. The length of that tile is usually linked to the printing plate or cylinder, which is why the printer should give you the exact repeat size before you design. Build your tile inside that size and check that elements crossing the edge continue correctly on the opposite side.

  • Straight repeat: the tile repeats in a simple grid. Clean and easy to read, but can look rigid.
  • Half-drop repeat: each column is shifted by half a tile. It hides the grid and usually looks more natural on folded tissue.
  • Random-looking scatter: several logos and icons at different angles within one tile. Good for crumpled filler because some marks always land right way up.

Ask whether the cut sheet will start at a fixed point in the pattern. In most cases it will not, so expect part-logos along the edges and design so that does not matter.

2. Space the logo for folding

Tissue is folded around a product or scrunched as filler, so the customer sees only a section. Space your logo so that at least two complete marks appear on any visible area of a folded wrap. On small sheets, shrink the tile; on large garment sheets, a slightly bigger logo can work. Leave clear space between marks so the pattern reads as intentional rather than crowded. Rotating some marks by 90 or 180 degrees helps a logo read correctly however the sheet is folded.

3. Keep ink coverage light

Tissue is a thin, soft paper. Large solid areas add a lot of wet ink to it, which can cause wrinkling, curling, slow drying and a stiffer feel, and may make sheets stick or mark each other. That is why tissue suppliers commonly limit or surcharge full-coverage designs, and some decline solid backgrounds altogether. Open, airy patterns with plenty of bare paper are cheaper, print more reliably and keep the tissue light. If you want a strongly coloured sheet, a dyed coloured tissue with a light print on top is often better than flooding white tissue with ink.

4. Limit colours and use spot references

  • Most custom tissue uses one or two colours. Each extra colour usually adds plate cost and registration risk.
  • Specify each colour as a Pantone spot reference. Expect the printed result on tissue to look softer and less saturated than on coated paper, because ink sinks into the fibres.
  • Avoid designs where two colours must align exactly; keep a small gap or overlap between colours to allow for registration movement.
  • On coloured tissue, inks are mostly transparent, so the paper colour changes the ink colour. Light inks on dark tissue may need an opaque or metallic ink; ask which are available and approve a sample.

5. Plan for show-through

Because tissue is translucent, the print is visible from the back, particularly with dark inks such as black, navy or deep red. This is a property of the paper, not a defect. Text will read in mirror image from the reverse side, and where sheets overlap, patterns stack and darken. If both sides will be seen, choose a lighter ink, reduce coverage, use a pattern that still looks good reversed, or wrap with two layers. Test a printed sample against your product, especially white or pale items, which can make dark print behind them more visible.

6. Prepare files correctly

  1. Supply vector artwork (AI, EPS or PDF) with fonts converted to outlines.
  2. Build the tile at 100% scale to the printer's repeat size, plus a mock-up of a full sheet showing how the tiles join.
  3. Name each colour with its Pantone reference and delete unused swatches.
  4. Keep lines and text bold; confirm the printer's minimum line weight and text size.
  5. Ask for a digital proof of the repeat, then a printed sample if colour or show-through matters.

Common mistakes

  • Placing one large logo in the centre of the sheet, which disappears when folded.
  • Sending a tile that does not join seamlessly, leaving visible lines in the repeat.
  • Using gradients, photos or fine type that fill in on soft paper.
  • Choosing a dark, heavy design without checking show-through on the real tissue.
  • Matching colour on screen instead of on a printed tissue sample.

Frequently asked questions

What is a repeat pattern on custom tissue paper?

It is a design tile that repeats seamlessly across the whole sheet, so the logo stays visible however the tissue is folded or crumpled. The printer usually sets the tile length because it is linked to the printing plate.

How much ink coverage can tissue paper take?

Keep coverage light with open patterns. Many suppliers limit or charge more for designs covering more than about half the sheet, because heavy ink can wrinkle and weaken thin tissue. Confirm the limit with your printer.

Why does my tissue paper design show through on the back?

Tissue is translucent, so dark inks are visible from the reverse side and text reads in mirror image. Use lighter inks, lower coverage or two layers if both sides will be seen.

How many colours can I print on custom tissue paper?

Most custom tissue uses one or two flat spot colours. Avoid gradients and photos, specify Pantone references and approve a printed sample, because colours look softer on tissue.
