Reverse Tuck End vs Straight Tuck End vs Auto-Lock Bottom: Which Folding Carton Style?

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Choose a reverse tuck end (RTE) for light products when you want an economical, widely used carton. Choose a straight tuck end (STE) when the front panel must look clean and both end flaps should open in the same direction. Choose an auto-lock bottom with a tuck top when the product is heavier, glass, or packed by hand at volume. “Auto-lock” and “crash-lock” are usually two names for the same pre-glued bottom, but confirm the exact construction on the dieline.

The styles, explained

Reverse tuck end. The top closure flap is hinged on one main panel and the bottom closure flap on the opposite panel, so the two tucks fold in from opposite directions. Because the flaps sit on opposite sides, the flat blanks tend to nest efficiently on the press sheet, which is one reason RTE is a common default for cosmetics, supplements and pharmaceutical cartons. The trade-off is that one closure’s cut edge sits on the front face.

Straight tuck end. Both the top and bottom closure flaps are hinged on the same panel, normally the back, so both close toward the front from the same direction. The front panel stays uninterrupted at both ends, which suits retail display and window cartons. The layout often uses slightly more board than RTE.

Auto-lock bottom / crash-lock bottom. The bottom flaps are pre-folded and glued at the factory. When the flat carton is squared up, the bottom forms and locks by itself. The top normally closes with a tuck flap. It is stronger than a tuck bottom and much faster to erect by hand. Most suppliers use the two names interchangeably; a few use “crash-lock” for a variant that is only partly pre-glued, which is why the dieline matters more than the name.

Snap-lock (1-2-3) bottom. Often confused with auto-lock, this bottom is not pre-glued: the packer folds and interlocks the bottom flaps by hand. It is stronger than a tuck bottom and needs no extra gluing, but it is noticeably slower to assemble.

Decision rules

  • Light product, cost matters most (lipstick, serum, tablets): reverse tuck end.
  • Front graphics or a window must look clean on shelf: straight tuck end.
  • Heavy, dense or glass product, or anything that could push through a tuck bottom when lifted: auto-lock bottom with tuck top. As a practical test, if you would hesitate to pick up the filled carton with only the bottom tuck holding the weight, the bottom needs a lock.
  • Hand packing at volume: auto-lock bottom saves the most time per carton because the bottom forms itself.
  • Automatic cartoning line: tuck end styles are the usual choice; confirm the exact style, flap direction and tolerance with the machine supplier before approving the dieline.
  • Tamper evidence needed: any style can take a perforated tear strip or a seal label; specify it at RFQ stage because it changes the dieline.

Details buyers often miss

Artwork orientation. On a reverse tuck end, the top and bottom flaps come from opposite panels, so text on the end flaps must be placed in the correct direction on the dieline. Check the folded mock-up, not just the flat artwork. Our artwork and dieline preparation guide explains the checks.

Friction and slit locks. Tuck flaps can be fitted with small slit locks or friction cuts so they stay closed during handling. They are worth asking for if the carton is opened and closed several times.

Board choice. Carton style and board work together: a heavier product may be fine in a tuck end box made from thicker board, or may need a lock bottom on standard board. Test with the filled product.

Dimensions. All these styles are usually specified by inner L×W×H, with the tuck end as the opening. See the tuck end box dieline for how the panels and flaps are laid out.

What to send in your RFQ

  1. Carton style by name, plus a sketch or photo showing which side opens.
  2. Inner dimensions (L×W×H, mm) and filled product weight.
  3. Product type: glass, liquid, powder or solid, and whether it ships inside a shipper carton.
  4. Packing method: by hand or automatic cartoner (with machine model if known).
  5. Board type and thickness if decided, printing colors and finishes.
  6. Extras: hang tab, window, tear strip, insert.
  7. Quantity (MOQ 500 PCS) and target delivery date.

Common mistakes

  • Putting a heavy glass jar in a tuck bottom carton because the sample looked fine empty.
  • Calling a snap-lock bottom “auto-lock” in an RFQ, then receiving cartons that need hand folding, or a quote with unexpected gluing.
  • Approving flat artwork without checking end-flap text direction on a folded sample.
  • Changing carton style after a cartoning machine trial without telling the machine supplier.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between reverse tuck end and straight tuck end?

On a reverse tuck end the top and bottom tuck flaps are hinged on opposite panels and fold in from opposite directions. On a straight tuck end both flaps are hinged on the same panel, which keeps the front panel clean at both ends.

Is auto-lock bottom the same as crash-lock bottom?

In most supplier catalogs, yes: both describe a pre-glued bottom that locks into place when the flat carton is squared up. A few suppliers use the names differently, so confirm on the dieline. A snap-lock or 1-2-3 bottom is different because it is folded by hand and not pre-glued.

Which folding carton style is cheapest?

Reverse tuck end is commonly the most economical because its blanks nest efficiently on the sheet and it needs only a side seam glue. The actual cost difference is confirmed in the quotation.

When do I need an auto-lock bottom?

Use an auto-lock bottom when the product is heavy, dense or glass, or when cartons are packed by hand at volume and assembly speed matters. Confirm the choice with a filled sample.
