E-Flute vs B-Flute Pizza Boxes: Board, Venting and Grease Barrier Against Soggy Crust

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

B-flute is the usual choice for delivery pizza: its thicker corrugation (commonly about 2.5–3.2 mm) insulates better, stacks better in delivery bags and resists crushing on larger sizes. E-flute (commonly about 1.1–1.6 mm) prints more sharply and folds neatly, so it suits smaller, premium or carry-out pizzas that travel a short distance. Sogginess comes from trapped steam, so pair either board with lid or side vents that let vapour out, an unbleached or grease-resistant inner surface, and enough corrugation under the pizza to absorb some moisture. Confirm the combination with a real delivery test on a sample.

Why pizza goes soggy in the box

A freshly baked pizza releases a lot of steam. In a closed box, that vapour condenses on the lid and the base, and the crust underneath reabsorbs it. Oil from cheese and toppings soaks into the board at the same time, weakening it and marking the print. The box therefore has three jobs: hold heat, release steam, and keep oil from reaching the outer surface. Flute choice mainly controls heat and strength; vents and the inner surface control steam and grease.

Box size by pizza diameter is a separate decision, covered in our takeaway box size guide. This guide covers board and venting only.

Decision rules for flute

  • Choose B-flute for delivery, for pizzas of about 12 in and larger, for heavy toppings, and whenever boxes are stacked several high in a bag.
  • Choose E-flute for small or personal pizzas, quick carry-out, slices, or when detailed full-colour artwork matters more than insulation.
  • Consider a heavier construction (a stronger liner or a double-wall board) for very large or party-size pizzas that sag when lifted.
  • Do not choose by thickness alone: the liner papers matter too. Ask for the full board construction and compare on samples. Our corrugated packaging materials page explains the basics.

Venting options

Venting lets steam escape before it condenses. The trade-off is heat: more open area means a drier crust but a cooler pizza. Common options include:

  • Lid vents: small punched or push-out holes near the back or front of the lid. Push-out tabs stay closed in storage and are opened at packing.
  • Side or front vents: openings in the side walls or front flap that let vapour leave at the edges, where it rises without dripping back onto the pizza.
  • Raised base features: embossed ribs or a corrugated inner pad that lift the crust slightly so steam can travel underneath rather than soaking into it.
  • Loose base liner: a grease-resistant paper sheet or ridged circle placed under the pizza to absorb moisture and protect the base.

Two to four small vents is a common starting point for a standard delivery box, but the right number depends on pizza style, delivery time and climate. Thin-crust and Neapolitan-style pizzas usually need more venting than thick pan styles. Test with a timed delivery run rather than judging on a box opened at the counter.

Grease barrier and inner surface

Many pizza boxes use an unbleached kraft inner liner, which tends to hide oil marks and absorb some moisture, with a white or printed outer liner for branding. For oily pizzas, a grease-resistant coating on the food side, or a separate liner sheet, reduces oil wicking through to the print. Keep in mind that a fully sealed, plastic-like inner surface stops absorption and can make condensation worse, so balance grease resistance with breathability. If your market has rules on food-contact materials or on fluorinated grease treatments, request the relevant documents for your exact specification. For delivery packaging more broadly, see packaging for food delivery.

What to send in an RFQ

  1. Pizza style (thin, Neapolitan, pan, deep dish) and the sizes you sell.
  2. Main channel: dine-in leftovers, carry-out or delivery, and typical delivery time.
  3. Preferred flute (E, B or a request for a recommendation) and how many boxes are stacked per bag.
  4. Venting: number, position and whether you want push-out tabs.
  5. Inner surface: kraft, white, grease-resistant coating or separate liner sheet.
  6. Print method and coverage, inside and outside.
  7. Quantity per size; custom orders start from 500 PCS.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing E-flute for large delivery pizzas because the print looks better, then seeing crushed lids in stacked bags.
  • Adding a heavy grease barrier everywhere and creating a steam trap.
  • Testing a box at the counter instead of after a real delivery time.
  • Placing vents directly above toppings so condensation drips back onto the pizza.
  • Comparing quotes on flute name alone without the full board construction.

Frequently asked questions

Is E-flute or B-flute better for pizza boxes?

B-flute is usually better for delivery and larger pizzas because it insulates and stacks better. E-flute suits smaller pizzas, short trips and detailed print.

How thick is B-flute compared with E-flute?

B-flute is commonly about 2.5 to 3.2 mm thick, while E-flute is commonly about 1.1 to 1.6 mm. Exact thickness varies by supplier, so confirm on a sample.

How many vents should a pizza box have?

Two to four small vents is a common starting point for a standard delivery box, adjusted for pizza style, delivery time and climate after a real delivery test.

How do I stop grease showing through a pizza box?

Use an unbleached kraft or grease-resistant inner surface, or a separate liner sheet under the pizza, while keeping enough breathability to avoid trapping steam.
