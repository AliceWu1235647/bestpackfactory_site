Digital vs Rotogravure Printing for Custom Pouches: MOQ, Cost, Colour and Lead Time

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Digital printing needs no plates or cylinders, so it suits short runs, many SKUs and fast launches, but the cost per bag stays relatively high as quantity grows. Rotogravure engraves one metal cylinder per colour, which adds a one-time cylinder cost and lead time, but the cost per bag falls sharply on long runs and colour is very consistent on reorders. Typically, digital wins for a few thousand bags per design or less and gravure for long, stable runs; the crossover is often somewhere around 5,000–10,000 bags per design, but it depends on size, colours and structure, so compare both quotes at your real quantities.

How each process works, briefly

Rotogravure (often shortened to gravure) prints from metal cylinders engraved with tiny cells. Each cylinder carries one colour; ink fills the cells and transfers to the film. The film is usually printed on the reverse side (reverse printing) and then laminated, so the ink is buried between layers. Because the cylinders are durable and reusable, gravure is a long-run process built for large volumes and repeat orders.

Digital printing for flexible packaging commonly uses liquid-toner or inkjet presses that print the image directly from the file onto film. There is no cylinder to make, so the first bag costs roughly the same to print as the thousandth. The printed film is then laminated and converted into pouches like any other roll. Print and lamination options for digital and gravure jobs are confirmed in the quotation.

A third process, flexographic printing, sits between the two in setup cost and is common for simpler designs; it is worth asking about if neither option fits.

Decision rules

  • Choose digital when you are launching a new product, testing a market, running many flavours or SKUs in small quantities, using seasonal or personalised designs, or need bags quickly.
  • Choose gravure when one design sells in large, stable volumes, when you want the lowest cost per bag over a year, when brand colours or metallic effects must match exactly every time, or when your artwork rarely changes.
  • Start digital, move to gravure when a SKU proves itself. This is a common path: launch on digital, then switch the best sellers to gravure once volumes justify cylinders.
  • Keep digital for the long tail even after moving top sellers to gravure; the low-volume SKUs rarely pay back cylinder costs.

How to compare quotes fairly

  1. Ask for both processes at the same size, structure, finish and zipper, at two or three quantities (for example 1,000, 5,000 and 20,000 per design).
  2. For gravure, separate the one-time cylinder cost from the unit price, then calculate total cost per bag at each quantity.
  3. Add up the expected reorders for the year. Cylinders are paid once, so gravure looks better when you count repeat orders with unchanged artwork.
  4. Count designs, not just bags. Ten SKUs of 1,000 bags is a very different job from one SKU of 10,000 bags.
  5. Ask about lead time for the first order and for reorders separately.

Colour expectations

Neither process prints colour identical to your screen. For gravure, each spot colour is mixed and matched to a target, so a brand colour can be held very closely. For digital, spot colours are usually simulated from process or extended-gamut inks; most colours land close, but some saturated oranges, greens, blues and metallics may not match exactly. If a colour is critical, say so in the RFQ and approve a printed proof or pre-production sample. Our existing guide on flexible packaging laminate structures explains how the film behind the ink (clear, white, metallised or foil) also changes how colours appear.

What to send in your RFQ

  • Pouch format, size and material structure
  • Number of designs and quantity per design
  • Number of colours, any Pantone spot colours and any metallic or white ink
  • Finish: gloss, matte or spot matte
  • Expected reorder frequency and whether artwork will change
  • Target delivery date

If you are unsure which process fits, ask for both in the same quotation. For product options, see flexible packaging and custom stand-up pouches; coffee buyers can also read our short answer on gravure vs digital for coffee bags.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a digital unit price with a gravure unit price while ignoring cylinder cost.
  • Ordering gravure cylinders for a product whose artwork or regulations are still changing.
  • Expecting an exact metallic or neon match on digital without a proof.
  • Forgetting that each new flavour on gravure usually needs at least some new cylinders.
  • Approving colour on a monitor instead of a printed sample.

Frequently asked questions

Is digital printing cheaper than gravure for pouches?

For short runs and many designs, digital is usually cheaper overall because there are no cylinders. For long runs of one design, gravure usually becomes cheaper per bag once the cylinder cost is spread over the volume.

What is a gravure cylinder cost?

It is a one-time charge for engraving one metal cylinder per printed colour. Cylinders are typically reused on reorders as long as the artwork for that colour does not change.

Can digital printing match Pantone colours on flexible packaging?

Many Pantone colours can be simulated closely with process or extended-gamut inks, but some saturated and metallic colours may not match exactly. Approve a printed proof if colour is critical.

When should I switch from digital to gravure?

Commonly when a single design sells in stable, large volumes with repeat orders and unchanged artwork. Compare total cost including cylinders at your expected annual quantity.
