China vs Domestic Custom Packaging for 500 Units: An Honest Decision Guide

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

At 500 units, a domestic supplier is often the better choice for simple, fast-changing or urgent packaging, because fixed costs such as freight minimums, customs clearance and duties weigh heavily on a small order. Ordering from China tends to make sense when the box is structurally complex or heavily finished, the design is stable, you can plan around a longer lead time, or you can consolidate quantity. Decide on landed cost per usable box and on timing risk, not on the factory unit price.

Why 500 units is the hard case

Every custom packaging order carries two kinds of cost. Variable costs grow with each box: board or paper, ink, coating, conversion labour and the box's share of freight volume. Fixed costs are paid once per order or per design: cutting dies, printing plates or press setup, sampling, freight minimum charges, customs entry and broker fees, and your own time managing the order.

At 5,000 units the fixed costs disappear into the unit price. At 500 units they can be a large share of the total. That is why a low per-box quote from an overseas factory can still lose to a higher domestic quote once everything is added up, and why the same comparison can flip completely at 2,000 units. Choose a domestic supplier when…

  • You need boxes in a few weeks. International production plus transit rarely fits an urgent launch, and air freight to rescue a schedule can erase any unit-price saving.
  • The box is simple. A one- or two-colour printed mailer or a plain kraft box with a logo has little labour content, so the overseas conversion-cost advantage is small and the fixed import costs dominate.
  • The design is still changing. If you expect to revise artwork after the first run, smaller, faster domestic batches reduce the risk of being stuck with obsolete stock.
  • You reorder small quantities often. Each overseas reorder repeats freight and customs steps; a local supplier can top up stock with little overhead.
  • You want to inspect production in person or your customer requires a domestic origin.

Choose a China factory when…

  • The packaging is complex. Rigid boxes, magnetic closures, custom inserts, foil stamping, embossing and soft-touch lamination involve more processes and more handwork, which is where overseas conversion costs typically make the biggest difference.
  • The design is stable. Once artwork and structure are proven, a larger single order lowers the fixed cost per box.
  • You can plan ahead. If packaging is ordered well before stock runs out, the longer lead time stops being a risk.
  • You can consolidate. Combining several SKUs, boxes plus labels or tissue, or a larger quantity into one shipment spreads freight and customs costs.
  • One factory can handle the whole job. At BestPackFactory every product is made in our own factory, with an MOQ of 500 PCS, so one supplier can quote the box and related items together.

A five-step way to decide

  1. Fix one specification. Write identical dimensions, board, print, finish and packing requirements so both quotes describe the same box. A mailer box dieline helps you lock the structure.
  2. Request quotes at 500, 1,000 and 2,000 units from each side. How fast the unit price falls tells you how much of it is fixed setup cost.
  3. Add every landed-cost line to the overseas quote: freight to your door, customs clearance, broker fees and the duty rate that applies to your product's HS code on the day you import. Duty rules have changed several times in recent years, so confirm the current position with your broker rather than relying on an old quote.
  4. Put a price on time. Estimate what a late or reprinted order would cost you in lost sales or rush freight, and weigh it against the saving.
  5. Compare landed cost per usable box, after allowing for samples and an overage or spoilage margin, and decide per SKU. Many brands end up using both: domestic for fast-changing items and an overseas factory for stable, complex packaging.

What to send in an RFQ so the comparison is fair

  • Inner dimensions in millimetres or inches, and the product weight
  • Box style, board grade or thickness, and flute if corrugated
  • Print method expectations, colours (CMYK or Pantone) and print sides
  • Finishes such as lamination, foil or embossing, and any inserts
  • Quantities: 500, 1,000 and 2,000
  • Delivery address and the Incoterm you want quoted (for example EXW, FOB or DDP)
  • Target in-hand date and whether a physical sample is needed first

Common mistakes

  • Comparing an ex-works overseas price with a delivered domestic price.
  • Forgetting freight minimums and customs fees on a small shipment.
  • Ignoring sampling time and cost when planning a launch date.
  • Choosing on price alone for a design that will change in three months.
  • Assuming domestic is always simpler; some domestic suppliers outsource complex boxes themselves, so ask where the work is actually done.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to order 500 custom boxes from China than from a domestic supplier?

Not automatically. At 500 units, fixed costs such as setup, sampling, freight minimums, customs clearance and duties are spread over very few boxes, so the landed cost per box can end up close to, or above, a domestic quote. Compare landed cost per usable box, not the factory unit price.

When does a domestic packaging supplier make more sense?

Domestic usually wins when you need the boxes within a few weeks, when the design is simple and likely to change, when you reorder small quantities often, or when you want to inspect production in person.

When does ordering packaging from China make more sense?

China tends to make sense when the structure or finish is complex, such as rigid boxes, inserts, foil and embossing, when the design is stable, when you can plan around a longer lead time, or when you can combine several items or a larger quantity into one shipment.

Should I compare quotes at more than one quantity?

Yes. Ask both suppliers to quote the same specification at 500, 1,000 and 2,000 units. The way the unit price falls as quantity rises shows how much of the price is fixed setup cost, which is the key input for the decision.
