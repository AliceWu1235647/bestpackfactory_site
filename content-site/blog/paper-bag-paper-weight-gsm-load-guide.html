Paper Bag GSM and Load: What Paper Weight and Handle a Shopping Bag Needs for What It Carries

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Match the paper to the load, then reinforce where bags actually fail: the handle anchor and the bottom. As a typical starting point, machine-made kraft bags use about 80–100 gsm for loads under 1 kg, 100–120 gsm up to roughly 1.5–2 kg, and 120–150 gsm with a glued handle patch and a bottom board for heavier loads. Luxury coated bags usually use heavier art paper or card (often 157–250 gsm) for stiffness and print, with rope or ribbon handles and board reinforcement. Gsm alone does not guarantee strength, so confirm with a load test on pre-production samples.

Why gsm is not the whole story

Grammage tells you how heavy a square metre of paper is, not how strong it is. Strength depends on fibre type and length, how the paper was made, and how the bag is constructed. Virgin kraft paper with long fibres is typically tougher in tear and tensile strength than coated art paper of the same weight, which is why a 120 gsm kraft bag can carry more than a 157 gsm coated bag. Coated papers are chosen for print quality and stiffness; their strength usually comes from lamination and board reinforcement rather than from the paper itself.

Bag size matters too. A large bag gives more room to overfill it, and wide panels flex and tear more easily than narrow ones. If you are still deciding dimensions, start with the paper bag sizes and handle types guide, then come back here to set the paper weight for the load.

Handle type and how it is anchored

  • Twisted paper handles are glued inside the bag between the wall and a reinforcement patch. Most failures are at the anchor, not the handle, so a wide, well-glued patch matters more than the handle itself.
  • Flat paper handles are glued along a strip on the inside of the opening. They are economical and suit lighter takeaway and grocery loads; check that the glued strip is long enough for the weight.
  • Rope, cotton or ribbon handles on luxury bags pass through punched holes, so a board strip inside the folded top is normally used to stop the holes tearing. Knotted ends, eyelets or glued anchors are alternatives agreed with the factory.
  • Die-cut handles are cut directly into the bag top. They suit light loads unless the area around the cut-out is reinforced with a patch.

Reinforcement that adds real load capacity

  1. Handle patch: a kraft or board patch glued over the handle ends spreads the load across the panel.
  2. Top fold (turn-top): folding the top edge inward doubles the paper where the handles pull; luxury bags add a board strip inside it.
  3. Bottom insert board: a card or greyboard panel in the base stops the bottom sagging and spreads point loads such as jar or bottle bases.
  4. Lamination: film lamination on coated bags improves tear and moisture resistance as well as appearance.
  5. Glue coverage: side seams and bottom folds should be fully glued; partial glue lines are a frequent weak point under load.

How to load-test samples

Ask for pre-production samples in the final paper, handle and construction, then test them with your real product rather than relying on a number on a quotation:

  1. Static hang test: fill the bag with the intended load plus a safety margin agreed in your specification (many buyers test at around 1.5–2 times the normal load) and hang it by the handles for an agreed time.
  2. Lift or jerk test: lift the filled bag sharply several times to simulate a customer picking it up quickly.
  3. Carry test: walk with the filled bag for a few minutes, including stairs, to check swinging and handle comfort.
  4. Humidity check: paper weakens when damp; if bags are used outdoors or in humid climates, repeat a test after conditioning in similar conditions.
  5. Record the failure point: note whether the handle pulled out, the patch peeled, a side seam split or the bottom opened. Each points to a different fix.

Agree the pass criteria, the test load and the sample quantity before mass production, so the bulk order can be checked against the same standard.

What to send in your RFQ

  • Bag dimensions (width × gusset × height) and quantity.
  • What the bag will carry and its maximum weight.
  • Paper type preference (kraft, white kraft, coated art paper) and target gsm, or ask for a recommendation.
  • Handle type, colour and anchoring method.
  • Required reinforcements (handle patch, top board, bottom board) and finishes.
  • Your load-test method and pass criteria.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing gsm by price or feel without stating the carried weight.
  • Specifying heavy coated paper and assuming it is stronger than kraft.
  • Rope handles without a board strip in the top fold.
  • No bottom board for heavy or point-load contents such as jars.
  • Approving a sample by look only, without a load test.

Frequently asked questions

What gsm paper bag do I need for 2 kg?

A machine-made kraft bag of about 100–120 gsm with a well-glued handle patch is a common starting point for up to roughly 1.5–2 kg. Add a bottom board and test samples with your real product.

Is a higher gsm paper bag always stronger?

No. Kraft paper with long fibres is often stronger than coated art paper of the same weight. Construction, glue and reinforcement matter as much as gsm.

Where do paper shopping bags usually fail?

Most often at the handle anchor, where the handle or patch pulls away, and at the bottom fold. Reinforcing those two areas adds more capacity than simply raising gsm.

How should I load-test a custom paper bag?

Hang a filled sample by its handles with the normal load plus an agreed safety margin, lift it sharply several times, carry it, and record where any failure occurs.
