Why Lamination Peels, Bubbles or Lifts on Printed Boxes, and How to Prevent It

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Lamination fails when the film does not bond fully to the printed sheet. The usual causes are ink that was not dry enough before laminating, spray powder or dust on the print, too little adhesive or too little heat and pressure, film whose surface treatment has weakened with age, damp paper, and film stretched over folds and scores. When the failure appears tells you a lot: bubbles straight after lamination point to the sheet or process, while blistering or lifting days later points to trapped moisture or solvent, heat in transit or stress at folds. Check laminated samples by folding, edge-lifting and resting them before approval.

Two questions that narrow the cause

When did it appear? Problems visible at the factory, during die-cutting or gluing, usually come from the print surface or the laminating set-up. Problems that appear days or weeks later, often after shipping, usually involve gas or moisture that was trapped under the film, adhesive that had not fully cured before folding, or heat and humidity on the way. Where is it? Bubbles across flat solid areas, lifting at cut edges and lifting along folds each have different typical causes, as the table shows.

A simple diagnostic: where does the ink stay?

Lift the film at a failed area and look at both sides. If the film comes away clean and the ink stays on the board, the bond between film and ink failed; look at adhesive amount, heat and pressure, spray powder and film surface treatment. If the ink comes away on the film and leaves bare paper, the ink itself was not anchored to the paper, which points to drying, ink additives or a varnish or coating applied before lamination that the adhesive does not bond to well. If paper fibres tear away with the film, the bond is strong and the failure is more likely mechanical, such as folding stress.

Main causes in more detail

  1. Undried ink. Sheets laminated too soon after printing can release solvent or moisture under the film. Heavy coverage, such as full dark backgrounds or several overprinted colours, needs more drying time. Ask how long printed sheets rest before lamination on your job.
  2. Spray powder and dust. Offset printers often use anti-set-off powder to stop wet sheets marking each other. Too much, or a coarse grade, leaves particles that the adhesive cannot bridge.
  3. Adhesive, heat and pressure. Too little adhesive, low temperature or low nip pressure gives a weak bond; too much heat can cause shrinkage and bubbles. Heavy-ink areas usually need more adhesive than light areas.
  4. Film surface treatment. Plastic films are surface-treated so adhesives can wet them, and that treatment can weaken with long storage. Stored film should be within its usable period.
  5. Humidity and paper moisture. Damp paper releases moisture under heat. Very dry conditions can make board crack at folds.
  6. Folding and scoring. On folding cartons, the film bends over every score. A score that is too deep or narrow for the board, or folding before the adhesive has cured, can lift or crack the film. On rigid boxes the laminated wrap is stretched around corners and turned in at edges, which are the usual spots for lifting.

Prevention: what to specify

  • State the film type: gloss, matte, soft-touch or anti-scratch. Finish choice is covered in the soft-touch lamination guide and on the matte lamination page.
  • Flag heavy solid areas in the artwork so the printer plans drying and adhesive accordingly.
  • Avoid adding varnish or other coatings under the film unless the supplier confirms they are compatible with lamination.
  • Mark fold lines and any finishing on top of the film, such as spot UV or foil, so compatibility is checked before production. Folding carton print set-up is explained in the folding carton printing guide.
  • Tell the supplier the shipping route and season, especially for hot containers or very humid destinations.

Acceptance checks on arrival

  1. Visual check under raking light: hold boxes at a low angle to a lamp to show bubbles, haze and wrinkles.
  2. Fold test: fold a few cartons fully along each score and back, then look for lifting or cracking.
  3. Edge-lift test: try to start the film at a cut corner with a fingernail. A well-bonded film is usually hard to start and may take paper fibres with it.
  4. Rest test: keep a few samples in a warm room for a day or two and recheck for late blistering.
  5. Sample size: pull samples from several cartons and positions, not just the top layer. If your contract uses a sampling plan such as ISO 2859-1, apply it here.

Acceptable defect levels are agreed between buyer and supplier for each project. Record the agreed checks on the approved sample so both sides judge bulk the same way.

Common mistakes

  • Rushing printed sheets into lamination to save a day on a tight schedule.
  • Approving a digital proof without seeing a laminated, folded physical sample.
  • Adding a coating under the film late in the project.
  • Blaming the film when the ink is lifting with it, or the other way round.
  • Storing finished boxes in a hot loft or near a heater before use.

Frequently asked questions

Will bubbles in box lamination disappear on their own?

Some very fine haze can reduce as the adhesive cures, but distinct bubbles and blisters usually stay. Judge the result after the curing time the supplier specifies, not straight off the machine.

Why does lamination crack or lift along the folds?

The film is stretched every time the box folds. Scores that are too deep or narrow, folding before the adhesive has cured, very dry board or a weak bond over heavy ink all make lifting or cracking at folds more likely.

How can I tell if the film or the ink is the problem?

Lift the film at a failed area. If the ink stays on the board, the film-to-ink bond failed. If the ink comes away on the film, the ink was not anchored to the paper. Paper fibres on the film usually mean the bond was strong and the stress was mechanical.
