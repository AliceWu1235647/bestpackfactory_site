Soy, Water-Based or UV Ink: Which Makes Packaging More Sustainable?

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Ink is a small share of a package's weight, so switching ink usually changes the environmental profile less than choosing the substrate, pack size, lamination and coatings. Where ink does matter is in solvent emissions during printing, in how easily printed paper can be recycled, and in food-contact safety. As a rule, vegetable-oil (soy) inks suit offset printing on paper and board, water-based inks suit flexo on paper, corrugated and some films, and UV-cured inks give near-zero solvent emissions and durability but can be harder to remove in paper recycling. Choose the ink that fits the substrate and process, then make only claims you can document.

Paper vs film: which ink fits

  • Paper and paperboard (cartons, cards, rigid box wraps). Offset with vegetable-oil inks is a common choice. If you want the box to be recycled with paper, avoid a full plastic lamination and consider an aqueous (water-based) coating instead; the coating decision usually matters more than the ink.
  • Corrugated, paper bags and paper food packaging. Water-based flexo inks are widely used. Ask how the ink suits the inner coating and any food-contact requirement.
  • Plastic films (pouches, sleeves, flexible labels). Solvent-based gravure or flexo is still common for adhesion and detail; water-based and UV systems exist for some structures. Discuss the film, print method and run length together, as explained in our digital vs rotogravure printing guide.

Recycling and de-inking

Paper recycling mills remove ink so that fibres can be reused for new paper. How easily that happens depends on the ink system, the paper and any coatings. UV-cured and other highly cross-linked ink films are commonly reported to be harder to detach, and heavy ink coverage adds more material to remove. For packaging that will be recycled into brown grades such as corrugated, de-inking is often less critical. If recyclability is a key claim for your pack, ask the printer which ink system they will use and whether any deinkability or recyclability assessment is available for that combination.

Food contact is a separate question

An ink can be lower in emissions and still unsuitable for food packaging if components can migrate into the food. That is especially relevant for UV inks on the outside of thin packaging. Migration and compliance documents are covered in our food packaging ink safety answer; treat sustainability and food safety as two checks, not one.

Is it worth the cost?

For many paper projects the price difference between ink systems is small compared with board grade, finishing and setup, but it varies by printer and specification, and low-migration UV inks tend to cost more. The practical approach is to ask for the same quote with your preferred ink and with the printer's standard ink, then decide. If budget is tight, these choices commonly make a larger environmental difference than the ink itself:

  1. Right-size the pack to cut material and freight volume.
  2. Replace plastic lamination on paper packs with an aqueous coating where scuff resistance allows.
  3. Reduce flood coverage of dark colours, which uses more ink and can make recycling harder.
  4. Use mono-material structures where the product allows.
  5. Avoid mixing materials that cannot be separated by the consumer.

Limits of eco claims

"Printed with soy ink" is a factual statement only if the inks used qualify, and the SoySeal mark is a licensed trademark that should not be printed without permission. Broad statements such as "eco-friendly packaging" are risky: the US FTC Green Guides (16 CFR Part 260) caution against unqualified general environmental benefit claims, and EU Directive (EU) 2024/825 restricts generic environmental claims that are not substantiated. Specific, verifiable wording is safer than a general green message. BestPackFactory makes no general environmental superiority claim; the inks and coatings for your order are stated in the quotation so that you can decide what you can say on pack.

What to send in your RFQ

  • Packaging type, substrate and whether it is food contact.
  • Your preferred ink system, or the claim you want to make, so the right ink can be quoted.
  • Coatings and finishes, including any lamination you are willing to drop.
  • Target market for the claim and quantity per design (MOQ 500 PCS).

Common mistakes

  • Specifying soy ink for a plastic pouch printed by gravure.
  • Choosing an eco ink but keeping a full plastic lamination on a paper box.
  • Printing a green claim or logo without documents from the printer.
  • Assuming a low-emission ink is automatically safe for food contact.

Frequently asked questions

Does soy ink make packaging more sustainable?

It replaces part of the mineral oil in offset ink with renewable vegetable oil and commonly lowers solvent emissions, but ink is a small share of the package. Substrate, size and coatings usually matter more.

Which ink is best for printing on plastic pouches?

Solvent-based gravure or flexo inks are still common on films for adhesion and detail. Water-based and UV systems exist for some film structures; confirm with the printer for your exact film.

Are UV inks bad for recycling?

Not always, but UV-cured ink films are commonly reported to be harder to remove in paper de-inking. Ask the printer whether a recyclability or deinkability assessment exists for your combination.

Can I print 'eco-friendly' on my box if it uses water-based ink?

A general claim like that is risky under the FTC Green Guides and EU Directive (EU) 2024/825. Use a specific, documented statement instead, and keep the supplier's ink details on file.
