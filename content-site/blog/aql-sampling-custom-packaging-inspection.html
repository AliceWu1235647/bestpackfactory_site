AQL Sampling for Custom Packaging: What It Means and How Many Boxes or Pouches to Check

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

AQL (acceptance quality limit) is the defect rate you agree to tolerate when you inspect a random sample instead of every unit. Under the widely used ISO 2859-1 single sampling plan at General Inspection Level II, a 500 pcs lot is checked with 50 units, 1,000 pcs with 80, 3,000 pcs with 125 and 5,000–10,000 pcs with 200. Many packaging buyers accept zero critical defects, use AQL 2.5 for major defects and AQL 4.0 for minor defects, and write all of this into the order before production starts.

What AQL actually means

AQL stands for acceptance quality limit in ISO 2859-1 (older documents say "acceptable quality level"). It is not a promise that the delivered lot will contain exactly that percentage of defects, and it is not a pass rate. It is a reference point that selects a sampling plan: a sample size and two numbers. If the inspector finds defects in the sample up to the accept number (Ac), the lot passes; if the count reaches the reject number (Re), the lot fails.

Three things decide the plan: the lot size (how many units of one design were produced), the inspection level (General Level II is the usual default), and the AQL value chosen for each defect class. The sample size comes from the lot size and level; the AQL only changes how many defects you tolerate in that sample.

The same single sampling plans also appear in ANSI/ASQ Z1.4, which is why inspection reports often quote either standard. ISO published a revised edition of ISO 2859-1 in 2026 that reorganises the tables and adds skip-lot rules, so name the edition you want used in your order and check the exact plan against it.

Classify packaging defects before anyone inspects

The AQL numbers only work if both sides agree what counts as critical, major and minor. Write a short defect list for your specific box or pouch and attach it to the purchase order. A typical split looks like this:

Note that "zero critical defects" is a buyer convention, not a row in the ISO table. Your sample size stays the same; you simply agree that one critical defect is enough to reject or hold the lot.

How to pick which cartons to open

  1. Inspect a finished lot. A final random inspection is usually done once production is complete and at least most of the order is packed, so the sample represents what will ship.
  2. Treat each design as its own lot. Five SKUs of 1,000 boxes each are five lots of 1,000, not one lot of 5,000, because a fault in one artwork does not tell you about the others.
  3. Choose master cartons at random. Pull from the top, middle and bottom of pallets and from different production days. A common rule of thumb used by many inspectors is to open roughly the square root of the number of master cartons, then draw sample units evenly from those cartons.
  4. Count defective units, not defects. If one box has a scuff and a misprint, record it once under its most serious class.
  5. Keep special tests separate. Drop, compression, seal-strength or burst tests destroy samples, so they normally use a much smaller special inspection level (S-1 to S-4) or a fixed test quantity agreed in advance.

Decision rules

  • Choose General Level II for routine orders. Level I (smaller samples) suits a long-running, stable item; Level III (larger samples) suits a first order or an item with a history of problems.
  • Choose a tighter major AQL (1.5 or 1.0) when the packaging goes straight to a retail shelf, carries a regulated label or is part of a premium gift set.
  • Stay at 2.5 / 4.0 for shipping cartons, inner trays and other items customers do not see.
  • Plan the failed-lot route in advance: agree whether the supplier sorts and replaces defective units before a second inspection, and who pays for it. These terms are confirmed in the quotation or order contract.

What to put in your RFQ or purchase order

  • Standard and edition, inspection level, and AQL per defect class (for example: ISO 2859-1, Level II, critical 0, major 2.5, minor 4.0).
  • Your written defect list with photos of acceptable and unacceptable examples.
  • The approved reference: signed sample or proof, colour tolerance and dimensions. Our sample approval checklist covers what to lock before mass production.
  • Who inspects (your own staff, a third-party inspector or the supplier's report), when, and what happens if the lot fails.
  • Any functional tests and their sample quantities, such as closure tests for custom boxes or seal and zipper checks for stand-up pouches. For lab tests, see the packaging testing standards guide.

Common mistakes

  • Reading AQL 2.5 as "2.5% defects allowed in the shipment". The plan accepts or rejects lots based on a sample; the real defect rate of an accepted lot can be higher or lower.
  • No defect list. Without one, a misaligned foil stamp can be argued as minor by one side and major by the other.
  • Sampling only the top cartons. Problems that start mid-run, such as glue drying out or ink drift, hide in the middle of the pallet.
  • Using the total order quantity as the lot size when several designs or materials are mixed.
  • Inspecting against the wrong reference. Always compare to the signed sample, not to a screen image.

Frequently asked questions

What does AQL 2.5 mean for packaging?

It means major defects are judged with the AQL 2.5 column of the sampling table. For a lot of 1,000 boxes at General Inspection Level II, that is a sample of 80 units: 5 or fewer major defects passes and 6 or more fails. It does not guarantee that only 2.5% of the shipment is defective.

How many boxes should be inspected in a 500 pcs order?

Under ISO 2859-1 General Inspection Level II, a lot of 281 to 500 units uses a sample of 50. At AQL 2.5 the lot passes with up to 3 major defects and fails at 4.

Which AQL should I use for custom boxes and pouches?

A common choice is zero tolerance for critical defects, AQL 2.5 for major defects and AQL 4.0 for minor defects. Premium retail or regulated packaging often uses a tighter major AQL such as 1.5 or 1.0.

Does the AQL change the sample size?

No. The sample size is set by the lot size and the inspection level. The AQL only changes the accept and reject numbers for that sample.
