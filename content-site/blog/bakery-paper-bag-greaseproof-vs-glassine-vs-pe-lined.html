Greaseproof vs Glassine vs PE-Lined Paper Bags: Which Stops Pastry Grease Without Sogginess?

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

For warm, buttery pastries such as croissants that are eaten the same day, a greaseproof paper bag (or a kraft bag with a greaseproof inner ply) is usually the best balance: it holds back butter stains while still letting steam escape so the crust stays crisp. Glassine looks cleaner and resists grease well, but it is stiffer, can crack at folds and is not a moisture barrier. A PE-lined bag gives the strongest grease and moisture barrier, but it traps steam, so warm pastries can turn soft; it suits cooled, very oily or longer-held items better. Confirm the choice by bagging your own product on a sample.

The real trade-off: grease out, steam out

A bakery bag has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. It must stop fat from wicking through the paper and staining the print or the customer’s hands, and it must let water vapour leave so a crisp crust does not go soft. Any barrier strong enough to block all grease and moisture will also hold steam inside. That is why the “best” material depends on whether the product is warm or cooled, how much butter or oil it contains, and how long it stays in the bag.

Paper weight, size and handles are a separate decision, covered in our paper bag GSM and load guide. This article only deals with the grease barrier.

Greaseproof paper

Greaseproof paper gets most of its resistance from very finely refined fibres packed tightly together, which leaves few open channels for fat to travel through. Grades differ a lot, and suppliers commonly describe grease resistance with a KIT rating from the TAPPI T 559 test, where a higher number means better hold-out. Butter-rich laminated pastries usually need a higher rating than bread or plain rolls. Greaseproof is also a common choice as the inner ply of a two-layer bag, with printed kraft on the outside for strength and branding.

Ask how the grease resistance is achieved. Some markets now restrict fluorinated (PFAS) grease treatments in food-contact paper, so many buyers specify a fluorochemical-free grade and request the matching declaration for their market.

Glassine

Glassine is pressed between polished rollers until it becomes smooth, dense and slightly see-through. That density gives it good grease and air resistance and a premium, clean look, which is why it is popular for cookies, macarons and small pastries sold in tea shops and patisseries. The weak points are stiffness and fold cracking: sharp creases can open tiny cracks that let grease through, and glassine is not a reliable moisture barrier. It can also become brittle in very dry conditions and lose stiffness in humid ones.

PE-lined paper

A polyethylene layer on the food side gives the strongest barrier to both fat and liquid, and the PE can be heat-sealed for a closed bag. That makes it useful for fried snacks, very oily pastries, frozen or chilled goods and products that sit in the bag for hours. The same barrier stops steam escaping, so a croissant bagged straight from the oven tends to soften as condensation forms on the inside. If you use PE-lined bags for warm items, consider letting products cool first, adding vent holes or using a window or open top. Recycling acceptance for paper-plastic combinations varies, so check the local scheme in your sales market. For coating alternatives, see PE vs PLA vs water-based coatings.

Decision rules

  • Warm croissants and Danish, sold for same-day eating: greaseproof bag or kraft outer with greaseproof inner ply.
  • Cookies, macarons, small dry pastries on display: glassine, often as a flat or window bag.
  • Fried items, very oily fillings, or goods held for hours: PE-lined, ideally with venting for anything packed warm.
  • Bread and plain rolls: plain kraft or a lighter greaseproof grade is often enough.
  • Printed outside must stay clean: use a two-ply construction rather than relying on one sheet.

What to send in an RFQ

  1. The products going into the bag, whether they are packed warm or cooled, and how long they stay bagged.
  2. Preferred barrier (greaseproof, glassine or PE-lined) or a request for a recommendation.
  3. Any target grease rating to verify on samples, and whether a fluorochemical-free grade is required.
  4. Bag style (flat, satchel with gusset, window bag), size and paper colour.
  5. Print coverage and whether ink sits on the outer ply only.
  6. Destination market and any food-contact documents you need.
  7. Quantity per size; custom orders start from 500 PCS.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing PE-lined bags for oven-warm croissants and then blaming the recipe for soft crusts.
  • Approving a sample with a dry test item instead of the butteriest product on the menu.
  • Testing for only a few minutes when real customers carry the bag for an hour.
  • Creasing glassine sharply at folds, which can open micro-cracks.
  • Assuming a recycling claim without checking the local scheme in the sales market.

Frequently asked questions

Which paper bag is best for croissants?

For warm croissants eaten the same day, a greaseproof bag or a kraft bag with a greaseproof inner ply is usually the best balance, because it resists butter stains while letting steam escape.

Is glassine greaseproof?

Glassine resists grease well because it is pressed very smooth and dense, but it is not a moisture barrier and can crack at sharp folds.

Why do pastries go soggy in PE-lined bags?

The polyethylene layer blocks steam, so moisture from warm pastry condenses inside the bag and softens the crust. Cooling products first or adding vents helps.

How is grease resistance measured?

Suppliers commonly quote a KIT rating from the TAPPI T 559 test, where a higher number means better grease hold-out. Confirm the result on a sample with your own product.
