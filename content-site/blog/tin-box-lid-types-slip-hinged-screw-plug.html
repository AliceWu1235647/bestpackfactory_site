Tin Box Lid Types: Slip, Hinged, Sliding, Screw or Plug Lid for Tea, Mints, Candy and Cosmetics

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Choose the tin lid by how often the customer opens it and how tightly the product must stay closed. A slip lid is the simple, low-cost default for candy, biscuits and gift tins. A hinged lid suits mints and small items opened many times a day, because the lid cannot get lost. A sliding lid fits flat pocket tins for mints and solid balms. A screw lid gives the most secure reclose for balms, salves and spices on round tins. A plug lid, often combined with an outer slip lid on tea caddies, gives the tightest fit for aroma-sensitive tea and coffee. No tin lid is a hermetic seal on its own, so fragrant or moisture-sensitive products usually also need a liner or a sealed inner bag.

Why the lid matters more than the body

Tinplate walls block light, odour and moisture; the weak point is where the lid meets the body. The closure therefore decides how well the product keeps after first opening, how the pack feels in the hand, and which shapes are realistic. If you are still deciding between a tin, a pouch and a carton for tea, read our tin vs pouch vs box comparison first; this guide assumes you have already chosen a tin and now need the right lid.

Tightness ratings are relative and assume a well-fitted tin. Real performance varies with tinplate thickness, tooling and liner, so confirm it on a sample.

Slip lid: the flexible all-rounder

A slip lid is the most widely used tin closure because it works with almost any outline, from a small round candy tin to a large rectangular biscuit box or a heart-shaped gift tin. Its seal depends on how closely the lid and neck are matched; a lid that is too tight frustrates the customer, while a loose one rattles and lets aroma escape. For food that must stay crisp, pair a slip lid with a sealed inner bag or a heat-sealed film.

Hinged lid: best for repeat daily opening

A hinged lid stays attached, which suits products the customer opens many times, such as mints carried in a bag or pocket. The trade-offs: the hinge limits printing near the hinge edge, the lid gives only a modest seal, and very large or unusual shapes are harder to make with a hinge.

Sliding lid: flat and pocket-friendly

Sliding tins are thin, flat containers where the lid travels along channels formed in the body. They stay flat in a pocket and open with one thumb. They are not designed to be airtight, so soft or melting products should be solid at room temperature and fit snugly. Ask the supplier to confirm the slide force on a sample: too loose and the tin opens in a bag; too stiff and it feels cheap.

Screw lid: the most secure reclose

Threads are rolled into both the lid and the neck, so this closure is limited to round tins. It is the usual choice for balms, salves, beard wax and pomade, because it will not pop open in a handbag or travel kit and it can take a foam or pulp liner to improve the seal. A wide, shallow screw tin can be hard to open with greasy fingers; a knurled lid edge helps.

Plug lid: the tea and coffee caddy closure

A plug lid pushes inside the opening rather than sitting over it, giving a firm friction fit. Tea caddies commonly use a double closure: an inner plug lid that seals the leaves and an outer slip lid that carries the printed design. This combination is one of the better options for aroma-sensitive tea and ground coffee.

Decision rules

  1. Tea, coffee or herbs that lose aroma: choose a plug lid or a double lid (inner plug plus outer slip lid), and still use a sealed inner bag if the tin will sit on a shelf for months. Our custom tea packaging bags can serve as the inner bag or a refill pack.
  2. Mints and small candy opened all day: choose a hinged or sliding lid so nothing is lost.
  3. Cookies, chocolates and seasonal gift tins: choose a slip lid; it allows the widest choice of shapes and a larger printed lid panel.
  4. Balms, salves and waxes: choose a round screw lid, with a liner if the product contains volatile oils.
  5. Products that must be kept away from children: ask specifically for a child-resistant tin design, and request the relevant test documentation before ordering.

Food and cosmetic contact

Most food and cosmetic tins have an interior lacquer between the product and the tinplate. Ask for the inner coating type and a declaration of compliance for your market; in the EU, food contact materials fall under Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004. Oily, acidic or salty products can attack an unsuitable coating, so test the actual product in the tin for several weeks before mass production.

What to send in your RFQ

  • Product, fill weight or volume, and whether it is dry, oily, powdered or solid
  • Preferred lid type and internal dimensions (diameter or length and width, plus usable height)
  • Whether you need an inner lid, liner, window, inner bag or tamper seal
  • Print method and finish expectations for lid, body and inside (plain, lacquered or printed)
  • Quantity per design and destination market for food or cosmetic documents

A standard size from existing tooling is usually faster to sample than a new shape; whether new tooling is needed for your design is confirmed in the quotation. See our custom tin boxes page for available formats.

Common mistakes

  • Expecting a slip or sliding lid to keep tea or coffee fresh without an inner seal.
  • Specifying outer dimensions only, then finding the product does not fit inside the neck.
  • Putting key artwork across the hinge line or the lid-body joint, where it is cut or hidden.
  • Approving the tin empty instead of filled, so lid fit and slide force are never checked with the real product.

Frequently asked questions

Which tin lid is most airtight?

Among common options, a screw lid with a liner or a plug lid with an outer cap usually reclose most tightly. No standard tin lid is fully hermetic, so sensitive products typically also use a sealed inner bag or film.

Is a hinged or sliding lid better for mints?

Both work well. A hinged lid is usually chosen for rectangular tins opened many times a day, while a sliding lid suits very flat pocket tins. Check the opening force on a filled sample.

Can a square tin have a screw lid?

No. A screw lid needs a threaded round neck, so square, rectangular and custom-shaped tins normally use slip, hinged, sliding or plug closures.
