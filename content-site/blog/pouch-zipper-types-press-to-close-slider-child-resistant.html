Pouch Zipper Types: Press-to-Close, Slider, Pocket, Child-Resistant or No Zipper

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Choose the reclosure by how the pack is used after opening. A press-to-close zipper suits most snacks, coffee and supplements; a slider zipper suits larger or heavy pouches and users who struggle with press tracks; a pocket zipper adds a tear-off strip for tamper evidence on big bags; a child-resistant zipper is for products that legally or ethically need it, and must be tested as a finished package. Choose no zipper for single-use, sachet or retort packs. Then place the tear notch above the zipper and pick a round or euro-slot hang hole only if the pouch will hang on a peg.

Decision rules

  • Choose press-to-close as the default when the pouch is opened several times and the product is not a fine powder. It is the most widely available option.
  • Choose a powder-resistant or double-track zipper for protein powder, flour or spices, where fine particles stop ordinary tracks from closing. Ask the supplier which profile they recommend and test it with your product.
  • Choose a slider when the pouch is large or heavy, when the user may have wet or gloved hands, or when older users need an easier closure.
  • Choose a pocket zipper when you want the zipper protected and a visible tamper-evident strip on large bags.
  • Choose a child-resistant zipper when your market's rules or your own risk assessment require it, for example for cannabis edibles. Confirm which test protocol your market accepts; in the United States, child-resistant packaging is commonly tested under the protocol in 16 CFR 1700.20.
  • Choose no zipper for single-dose packs, retort pouches and packs that are emptied in one use. It saves cost and keeps the seal area simple.

Child-resistant zippers: what buyers often miss

A child-resistant claim belongs to the complete package: film, zipper, seals, opening instructions and the way the user is expected to reclose it. A zipper on its own does not make a pouch child-resistant. When you buy, ask:

  1. Which finished package construction the test report covers, and whether your size and film match it.
  2. Which protocol and market the test refers to.
  3. Whether the pouch also needs opacity, smell resistance or tamper evidence under local rules.
  4. What opening instructions must be printed on the pack.

Documentation for any specific construction is confirmed in the quotation. For dimensions and artwork safe zones, start from the child-resistant pouch dieline, and see our child-resistant cannabis mylar bags for the product range.

Tear notch: placement and type

A tear notch is a small cut in the side seal that starts a clean tear. Commonly it is placed above the zipper, so the consumer tears off the top seal and then uses the zipper. That top seal also acts as basic tamper evidence: if it is torn, the pack has been opened.

  • Position: keep the notch far enough above the zipper that the tear line does not cut into the tracks; the exact distance is set on the dieline.
  • Both sides or one side: notches on both side seals let left- and right-handed users open the pack.
  • Laser scoring: some laminates tear unevenly. A laser-scored line guides the tear straight across. Ask whether it suits your structure.
  • Child-resistant packs: check whether a tear notch is allowed on your construction, because an easy-open feature can affect the test result.

Hang hole: round or euro slot

Add a hang hole only if the pouch will hang on a retail peg. A round hole is simple and fits most pegs. A euro slot is an oblong hole with a wider top that spreads the load and helps heavier pouches hang straight; it is widely used on retail pegs. A sombrero hole is a third style, shaped like a hat, offered by some suppliers. Both are punched in the top seal area above the zipper, so allow extra height on the dieline and keep text and logos out of that zone. Heavy pouches, roughly above 500 g, may tear at a hang hole; check this on a filled sample.

What to send in your RFQ

  1. Pouch format and size, including gusset
  2. Reclosure type: press-to-close, powder-resistant, pocket, slider, child-resistant or none
  3. Zipper position from the top edge, if your filling line needs a specific height
  4. Tear notch: one side or both, with or without laser scoring
  5. Hang hole: none, round or euro slot
  6. Product type, especially if it is a powder, oily or sharp
  7. Target market and any child-resistant or tamper-evidence requirement

Reclosures can be specified on most custom stand-up pouches; availability for each combination is confirmed in the quotation.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a standard zipper for a fine powder, then finding it will not close after the first scoop.
  • Placing the tear notch below or level with the zipper, so opening destroys the reclosure.
  • Assuming a "child-resistant zipper" makes the package compliant without a finished-package test.
  • Adding a slider or hang hole without adding height, which squeezes the front-panel artwork.
  • Skipping a fill-and-close test with the real product before approving production.

Frequently asked questions

Which zipper is best for powder pouches?

Fine powders often clog standard press-to-close tracks. Ask for a powder-resistant or double-track profile, or a slider, and test it by filling, scooping and reclosing a sample with your real product.

Is a child-resistant zipper enough to make a pouch child-resistant?

No. Child-resistance is tested on the finished package, including film, seals, zipper and opening method. Confirm which construction and protocol the test documentation covers.

Where should the tear notch go on a zipper pouch?

Commonly above the zipper, so tearing off the top seal opens the pack without damaging the zipper tracks. The exact distance is set on the dieline.

What is the difference between a round hang hole and a euro slot?

A round hole is simple and fits most pegs. A euro slot is an oblong hole with a wider top that spreads the load and helps heavier pouches hang straight. Both sit in the top seal area above the zipper.
