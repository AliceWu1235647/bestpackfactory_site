Why Ink Rubs Off Printed Kraft Boxes and Paper Bags, and How to Prevent It

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Uncoated kraft is rough and absorbent, so ink sits partly in and partly on top of loose fibres. Heavy dark solids, inks that are not fully dried or cured, and friction in transit can then leave the colour rubbing onto hands, products or the next carton. Prevention usually combines a rub-resistant ink suited to kraft, enough drying or curing time, a protective water-based coating or overprint varnish where the look allows, and less heavy solid coverage in high-contact areas. Agree a rub test, such as a Sutherland rub test with a set weight and number of strokes, and approve it on a printed sample.

Rub-off, also called scuffing or poor rub resistance, is a common complaint with printed kraft: dark logos leave marks on white products, bag fronts look worn after a day in the shop and cartons scuff each other in transit. It is not a sign that kraft is a poor choice; it is a property that needs to be specified. For how colours look on brown and white kraft in the first place, see our kraft paper packaging guide.

Why ink rubs off kraft

  • Rough, absorbent surface. Uncoated kraft has an open fibre surface. Part of the ink is absorbed, but on the high points of the fibres the ink film sits exposed and is the first to wear.
  • Chalking. On very absorbent stocks the ink's liquid part can be drawn into the paper so quickly that too little binder is left around the pigment on the surface. The dried print then rubs off as powder.
  • Heavy ink film on dark solids. Large areas of black, navy or deep brown need a lot of ink to look solid on kraft. Thicker films take longer to dry and have more material to transfer.
  • Incomplete drying or curing. Water-based and oil-based inks need time or heat to dry; UV inks need correct curing. Cartons that are converted, stacked or packed too soon can show set-off and rub marks later.
  • No protective layer. Uncoated kraft is often left without varnish for a natural look, which leaves the ink fully exposed.
  • Friction in use. Bags rub against clothing and counters; cartons vibrate against each other in transit. Fine dust and loose fibres act like an abrasive.

Decision rules

  • Choose an overprint varnish or water-based coating for dark solid designs on kraft boxes that will be handled a lot or shipped long distances.
  • Choose lamination for retail paper bags with large dark areas that will be carried and rubbed against clothing, if you can accept the film look.
  • Keep uncoated, unvarnished kraft when the print is mostly line art or a small logo and the natural feel matters more.
  • Redesign heavy solids where the product inside is white or light-coloured and could be marked.
  • Check food-contact needs if a coating will be on the inside or near food; the coating choice then has to be confirmed with documents. Our guide to soy, water-based and UV inks covers ink options.

How to test rub resistance

A widely used method is the Sutherland rub test, described in ASTM D5264. A printed sample is rubbed against a receptor sheet or another printed sample under a set weight for a set number of strokes, and the transfer and damage are assessed. Buyers commonly agree:

  1. The test method and whether it is dry or wet rub.
  2. The weight used and the number of strokes; typical choices vary by product, so set them with the supplier and confirm on a sample.
  3. What is rubbed against what: printed face against a plain sheet, or printed face against printed face as in a stack.
  4. How the result is judged, for example a visual rating against the approved sample.
  5. When the test is done: after the ink is fully dry or cured, not straight off the press.

A simple practical check is also useful: rub the printed area firmly with a clean white cloth or tissue several times and see what transfers. It is not a standard, but it quickly shows a problem worth discussing.

What to send in your RFQ

  • Product type: carton, mailer box or paper bag, and brown or white kraft.
  • Artwork with any large dark solids marked.
  • How the packaging will be used: retail carrying, e-commerce shipping, contact with light-coloured products.
  • Your preference on coating, varnish or lamination, or a request for the supplier's recommendation.
  • The rub test you want on the sample, with weight, strokes and acceptance method.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing full-coverage black on brown kraft with no coating, then shipping it in loose master cartons.
  • Judging rub resistance on a sample printed days earlier, then rushing production straight from press to packing.
  • Adding a coating late without checking whether glue, hot foil or a window patch still adhere to that area.
  • Testing only the printed face against a plain sheet when the real problem is cartons scuffing each other.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the ink on my kraft box rub off?

Uncoated kraft is rough and absorbent, so ink on the fibre high points is exposed. Heavy dark solids, ink that is not fully dried or cured, missing protective coating and friction in transit all make rub-off more likely.

How can I stop ink rubbing off paper bags?

Common options are a water-based coating or overprint varnish, rub-resistant inks, full drying or curing before converting, less heavy solid coverage, or lamination if you can accept the film look.

What is a Sutherland rub test?

It is a common abrasion test for printed materials, described in ASTM D5264. A printed sample is rubbed under a set weight for a set number of strokes and the transfer is assessed. Agree the weight, strokes and acceptance method with your supplier.

Does varnish change the look of kraft?

It can slightly change gloss and feel. Matte water-based coatings usually keep the natural look closest; approve it on a printed sample.
