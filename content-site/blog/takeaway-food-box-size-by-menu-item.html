Takeaway Box Sizes by Menu Item: Burgers, Fries, Fried Chicken, Pizza, Sandwiches and Meals

Written by Lisa Wu, Sales Manager & Packaging Project AdvisorPublished October 30, 2026Reviewed October 30, 2026

Lisa coordinates B2B packaging specifications, quotations, sampling and production communication with the BestPackFactory team.

Quick answer

Size each takeaway box from the real menu item, measured as it is served: wrapped, garnished and with any sauce pot. Add a few millimetres of clearance on each side, so the food does not touch the lid or get crushed, but not so much that it slides around. Pizza boxes follow pizza diameter in inches; burgers, sandwiches and chicken are sized by portion; meal boxes are sized by volume and compartments. Test every size with real food on a sample before ordering.

Sizing reference by menu item

These are typical starting points used in the market. Your portion, bun size and garnish decide the final dimensions, so treat the numbers as a first estimate to be confirmed on a sample.

Measure the food the way it leaves the kitchen

  1. Make the item exactly as served. Wrapped burger, full garnish, sauce cup, napkin or fork if they travel in the box.
  2. Measure length, width and height at the widest points with a ruler or calliper; for stacked burgers measure height after the top bun is pressed lightly, as it would be when the lid closes.
  3. Add clearance. A few millimetres per side is a common starting point; tall items need extra headroom so the lid does not touch toppings.
  4. Note what changes with the season or promotion. A limited-time double burger may not fit the regular box.
  5. Give inside dimensions (length × width × height) in your request, and say that they are inside measurements.

Item-by-item decision rules

Burgers

Choose a clamshell when staff need to box quickly with one hand; choose a tuck-top or windowed box for gourmet burgers where presentation matters. Height is where most burger boxes fail: a box that fits a single burger will crush a double. See burger packaging boxes.

Fries

Open scoop cartons work for dine-in and short trips; closed boxes keep heat in for delivery but can trap steam. Define each size by fill weight so portions stay consistent, then match the carton. See fries packaging boxes.

Fried chicken

Fried food releases steam and oil. Vents or holes help reduce sogginess, and a grease-resistant inner surface helps stop oil marks showing through the print. Size by piece count and decide whether sides travel in the same box. See fried chicken packaging boxes.

Pizza

Pizza boxes are normally corrugated board to keep the box rigid and give some insulation. Order the box by the pizza diameter, but check whether a quoted size refers to the inside or the outside of the box, because suppliers label this differently. Measure the baked pizza rather than the pan, and check the inside height against thick or loaded crusts. Pan, deep-dish or stuffed-crust pizzas typically need a deeper box, so give that height separately. See custom pizza boxes.

Sandwiches

Triangle-cut sandwiches suit wedge boxes; wraps, subs and baguettes need longer rectangular boxes. A window helps grab-and-go sales but adds a film panel to the specification. See sandwich packaging boxes.

Full meals

For rice, noodles and combination meals, size by volume and number of compartments, and check that wet and dry items stay apart when the box is carried at an angle.

Keep the size range short

Each box size is a separate printing and die-cutting job and takes separate storage space. Many restaurants cover their menu with three to six sizes by letting one box serve several items, for example one clamshell for single burgers and chicken sandwiches. Group items by similar footprint and height before you ask for quotes, and you will usually reduce cost and storage. Browse the full range on the food packaging page.

What to send in your RFQ

  1. A list of menu items with photos and inside dimensions for each box.
  2. Box style per item (clamshell, tuck-top, scoop, wedge, pizza box, compartment box).
  3. Whether the food is hot, oily, wet or cold, and how long it stays in the box (counter, takeaway, delivery).
  4. Need for vents, windows, compartments or a grease-resistant surface.
  5. Artwork and print colours, inside print if any.
  6. Quantity per size, food-contact documents needed for your market, and destination.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing from a supplier's stock list instead of your food. Stock sizes rarely fit a signature burger or a large chicken portion.
  • Giving outside dimensions as inside dimensions. Board thickness can take several millimetres from each side, more for corrugated board.
  • No headroom for toppings. Cheese and sauce stick to the lid and ruin the presentation.
  • Too much space. Food slides during delivery and arrives broken or cold.
  • No vents for fried food. Steam softens the coating and the box.
  • Skipping the food test. Always box real food on a sample and carry it the way a courier would.

Frequently asked questions

How much clearance should a takeaway box have?

A few millimetres per side is a common starting point, with extra headroom for tall items so the lid does not touch toppings. Confirm by boxing real food on a sample.

How are pizza boxes sized?

Pizza boxes are usually ordered by pizza diameter in inches, such as 10, 12 or 14 inch, with the inside length slightly larger than the pizza. Thick or loaded crusts need extra inside height.

Should I give inside or outside dimensions?

Give inside dimensions and label them as inside. Board thickness reduces the usable space, especially with corrugated board.

How many takeaway box sizes does a restaurant need?

Many restaurants cover their menu with three to six sizes by grouping items with a similar footprint and height. Fewer sizes usually means lower cost and simpler storage.
